The Book Begins:
Preface
Hello, world! I’m Nick Kremers, but you can call me the Stroked Out Sasquatch. It’s a name I earned after a massive hemorrhagic stroke tried to steal my life six years ago. Picture this: one minute you’re standing, talking, living, and the next, your brain decides to throw a party, and not the good kind. Mine was a real rager, complete with a craniotomy – that’s brain surgery, folks – and a lovely parting gift of 50 staples in my head.
Yeah, it was a rough scene. I was completely paralyzed on my left side. It felt like my life, as I knew it, was over. I’m not gonna sugarcoat it; I was terrified. But here’s the thing, and it’s a lesson I want to drill into your head right now: I found a way.
And you can too.
This isn’t some medical textbook written by some dude in a white coat who’s only seen stroke recovery in a chart. This is my story, my fight, my blueprint for clawing my way back from the abyss. Every stroke is different. Every body is different. Therefore, every recovery HAS to be different. What worked for me might need tweaking for you. But this book? It’s a starting point. It’s the map I wish I had when I was lying in that hospital bed, wondering if I’d ever walk again.
Think of it as a guide to help you create your own guide. Learn from the therapists, yes. They’re the experts. But don’t be afraid to experiment. Combine what they tell you with what YOU discover about your own body. Push the boundaries. Because let me tell you, sometimes, you gotta find your own way.
And you will. You’ve got this. The Stroked Out Sasquatch believes in you!
Part 1: Before the Storm: Life, Love, and a Loaded Past
Chapter 1: Big for My Age
From the moment I could stand, it seemed like everyone commented on my size. "You're big for your age," they'd say, a statement delivered with a mix of surprise and mild concern. I wasn't fat, not by a long shot, but I was tall, broad-shouldered, and generally built like a kid two or three years my senior. This physical presence, coupled with a naturally loud and boisterous personality, made me stick out in any crowd. I was a whirlwind of energy, always eager to explore, to laugh, and to be part of whatever was happening.
My enthusiasm found its perfect outlet in sports. I loved them all. Baseball, with its satisfying crack of the bat and the strategic dance between pitcher and batter, held a special place in my heart. Basketball, the fast-paced rhythm of dribbling and the satisfying swish of the net, electrified me. Football, the raw power and tactical complexity of the game, ignited a competitive fire within me. And soccer, with its fluid movements and the thrill of a perfectly placed goal, kept me constantly engaged.
It wasn't just a casual interest; I had a knack for athletics. My hand-eye coordination was good, my reflexes were quick, and my size gave me a distinct advantage. I could throw a baseball further, shoot a basketball higher, tackle a football player harder, and outrun most of my peers on the soccer field. I wasn't just participating; I was excelling.
Beyond the sports fields, I was generally considered a smart kid. I picked up on concepts quickly, had a decent memory, and possessed a natural curiosity that drove me to learn. I was also, for the most part, a people person. I got along with almost everyone, from the quiet kids in the back of the classroom to the popular kids on the playground. I had a knack for finding common ground and making people feel comfortable.
However, there was one glaring exception to my academic prowess: reading. The letters on the page seemed to swim and blur, and the stories felt like a tangled mess of words. While my friends devoured books, I struggled to decipher even the simplest sentences. It was a frustrating contrast to my athletic abilities and overall intelligence, a nagging reminder of my limitations.
In many ways, my size defined me. It was the first thing people noticed, the topic of countless conversations, and the reason I was often picked first for teams. But while my physical stature set me apart, it was sports that truly gave me a sense of belonging. The field, the court, the pitch – these were the places where my size wasn't just a curiosity, it was an asset. It was where I could channel my energy, test my limits, and find a sense of accomplishment. Sports were my outlet, my escape, and my way of proving that being "big for my age" wasn't just a description, it was a strength.
Part 2: The Day My Life Changed: The Stroke and Its Immediate Aftermath
Chapter 7: The World Tilts on Its Axis
December 3, 2018. A day that's burned into my memory, a scar on my soul. It started like any other. I was at a friend’s house, hanging out with my pregnant wife. We were chatting, laughing, living. Then, pop.
(A dramatic pause, a shift in tone)
It wasn't a loud bang, not like a gunshot. It was more... internal. Like a pressure cooker exploding inside my head. A split second of agony, and then... everything went sideways.
I tried to stand, but my legs wouldn't listen. They were like rubber, refusing to cooperate. I fell, hard. Panic started to claw its way up my throat. What the hell was happening to me?
(Voice tightens with emotion)
Lisa, my wife, she saw it. She saw the droop in my face, the slackness on my left side. She yelled, "You're having a stroke!"
I wanted to argue, to tell her she was wrong. But my words wouldn't come out right. Slurred, broken, foreign.
She grabbed her phone, ready to call 9-1-1. I tried to stop her. Pride, maybe. Stupidity, definitely. I thought I was fine. I wasn't fine.
(Voice softens, filled with gratitude)
Thank God she didn't listen to me. Thank God she made that call. From that point on, my memory gets… patchy. Fragments of images, sounds, feelings. I remember being on a gurney, a sea of faces above me. Family, friends, their faces a blur of worry. I tried to give them a thumbs-up, to reassure them. I hope it worked.
Then, darkness.

Looking back, I see that this period was a time of intense self-discovery, a period marked by both struggle and growth. She was beautiful, kind, and brought a unique dynamic to my life. Lisa, four years my senior at 40, brought a maturity and groundedness I found incredibly attractive. Her daughter, Shelby, 19, lived with her father.
Stroked Out Sasquatch says: Sometimes, the best things in life happen when you least expect them. Be open to new beginnings and embrace the unexpected.

My parents were building a shop house, a testament to their hard work and entrepreneurial spirit. It was a place where family could gather, memories could be made, and the legacy of their labor would endure. While it was under construction, Lisa moved in with me at my parents' house. I saw this period as a stepping stone, a foundation for the family I longed to build.

The news hit Lisa hard, leaving her reeling from grief and guilt. In the aftermath of the tragedy, I found myself drawn to her, offering comfort and support. Our connection deepened, and amidst the sorrow, a new kind of love blossomed.
In a whirlwind decision, after only two weeks of knowing each other, we were married. It was a quiet exchange of vows at the local courthouse, a simple ceremony devoid of fanfare. As I stood there, vowing to love and cherish Lisa, I couldn't shake the feeling that I was rushing into something monumental.
Stroked Out Sasquatch says: Impulsivity can lead to both great joy and significant challenges. It's important to balance passion with careful consideration.
Looking back, I realize that our marriage was built on a foundation of intense emotions and rapid decisions. The shadow of Eric's death loomed large, and the complexities of our blended family posed unique challenges. Yet, amidst the turmoil, there was also a profound love, a shared commitment to building a future together.
Chapter 6: A Family Forged and Doubts Raised
Our marriage began in a whirlwind of emotions – love, grief, and the weight of the unknown. We settled into a routine, or what passed for one, amidst the lingering shadow of Eric's death. Just a few short months after meeting, we moved into our own house, a significant step that further intertwined our lives. However, the initial rush of our hasty vows began to fade, revealing the underlying tensions and uncertainties.
Months after our quiet courthouse ceremony, Lisa announced she was pregnant. It was a shock, and I immediately questioned its validity. Given the circumstances and her previous beliefs about her fertility, I insisted she take a pregnancy test to confirm. The confirmation did little to quell my doubts.
The honeymoon phase, if it ever truly existed, was long gone. The stresses of blended family life, and Lisa's increasingly frequent and concerning seizures began to erode any semblance of stability. The cracks in our foundation were widening, and the tension was palpable.

Chapter 33: Mirror Therapy: Thinking Movement into Existence
Mirror therapy is a technique that can be a valuable tool in the early stages of stroke recovery, particularly for regaining movement in paralyzed limbs. It's a relatively simple concept, but it can have a profound impact on the brain's ability to "relearn" movement.
Here's how it works:
1.	Setup: You sit at a table with a mirror placed vertically between your arms. Your unaffected arm is placed in front of the mirror, while your affected arm is hidden behind it.
2.	Movement: You perform movements with your unaffected arm, watching its reflection in the mirror. The brain, seeing the reflection of the moving limb, is tricked into believing that the affected limb is also moving.
3.	"Thinking" Movement: This visual illusion can help to stimulate the brain pathways that control movement in the affected limb. It's a way to "think" movement into existence, even before actual movement is possible.
Here are some practical tips for implementing mirror therapy:
•	Start Simple: Begin with small, basic movements of the unaffected arm, such as bending and straightening the fingers or wrist.
•	Focus on the Reflection: Pay close attention to the reflection in the mirror, focusing on the illusion of movement.
•	Consistency is Key: Perform mirror therapy regularly, even for short periods. Consistency is more important than duration.
•	Combine with Other Therapies: Mirror therapy can be used in conjunction with other forms of therapy, such as physical and occupational therapy.
Mirror therapy is not a miracle cure, but it can be a helpful tool in the early stages of recovery. It's a way to tap into the brain's plasticity, its ability to rewire itself, and to promote the return of movement.
Stroked Out Sasquatch says: Mirror therapy is a testament to the brain's remarkable capacity for healing. It's a technique that can help you "think" movement back into existence, especially in the early stages of recovery. Be patient, be consistent, and trust in the process. Even small movements are victories, and they pave the way for greater gains.
Chapter 34: Regaining Movement: From Flinch to Function
Regaining movement after a stroke is a gradual process, a journey that often begins with the smallest of twitches and culminates in functional use of the affected limb. It's a journey that requires patience, persistence, and a deep understanding of the body's potential for recovery.
The progression of movement recovery often follows a pattern:
•	Starting with a flinch: In the early stages, the only sign of movement might be a small, involuntary muscle contraction, a flinch. This may seem insignificant, but it's a crucial starting point, a sign that the brain is still communicating with the muscle.
•	Working that flinch to exhaustion: Even a tiny flinch can be worked. Repeating the movement, even if it's just a flicker, to the point of exhaustion can help to strengthen the neural pathways and increase muscle activation.
•	Progressing to small movements: As the connection between the brain and the muscle strengthens, the flinch will evolve into a small, controlled movement. This might be a slight bend of the finger or a small twitch of the wrist.
•	Gradually increasing the range and complexity of movements: Over time, the range of motion and the complexity of the movements will increase. The small movements will become larger, more coordinated, and more functional.
Therapy bands can be a valuable tool in this process. They provide resistance, helping to strengthen the muscles as they begin to fire.
Negative pressure can also be used to facilitate muscle firing. This technique involves applying suction to the skin over the affected muscle, which can help to increase blood flow and stimulate muscle activation.
Here's a more detailed explanation of negative pressure:
•	Application: A device is used to create suction on the skin.
•	Mechanism: The suction increases blood flow to the area, which can help to deliver nutrients and oxygen to the muscle. It can also help to stimulate sensory receptors, which can, in turn, activate muscle fibers.
•	Use: Negative pressure can be used in conjunction with exercise to help improve muscle function.
Regaining movement is a long and challenging process. It requires consistent effort, patience, and a belief in the body's ability to heal and adapt.

Chapter 2: Understanding Stroke: Your Brain, Your Battle
The day my life took a detour, a hard left turn into the unknown, I didn't know what was happening. I just knew something was wrong. A pop in my head, like a firecracker going off, and then... nothing. Or, worse than nothing. A feeling of my body betraying me, refusing to cooperate.

And that's where knowledge becomes your weapon, your shield, your damn superpower in this fight.
What Exactly Is a Stroke?
Let's break it down, because "stroke" is a word we hear a lot, but do we really know what it means? Imagine your brain is a city, a bustling metropolis of thoughts, feelings, movements, everything that makes you you. Now, that city needs a constant supply of roads, right? Well, in this case, the roads are blood vessels, carrying oxygen and nutrients. A stroke is basically a traffic jam, or worse, a road collapse, in that city.   
There are two main ways this happens:
•	Ischemic Stroke: This is the most common type, accounting for about 85% of all strokes. Think of it as a roadblock. A blood clot, or thrombus, forms in an artery supplying blood to the brain, or travels there from somewhere else in the body. These clots can form in arteries already narrowed by fatty deposits (plaque), a condition called atherosclerosis. Or they can travel from elsewhere, like the heart, often caused by an irregular heartbeat (atrial fibrillation).   
•	Hemorrhagic Stroke: This is the road collapse. It's less common, but often more deadly. A blood vessel in the brain ruptures or leaks, spilling blood into the surrounding brain tissue. This blood puts pressure on the brain cells, damaging them. There are a couple of types of hemorrhagic strokes: 
o	Intracerebral Hemorrhage: This is when a blood vessel inside the brain bursts.   
o	Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: This involves bleeding between the brain and the tissues that cover it. It's often signaled by a sudden, severe headache.   
  
And then there's the "mini-stroke," or Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA). It's like a temporary road closure. Symptoms are similar to a full-blown stroke, but they don't last, and it doesn't cause permanent damage. But listen up: a TIA is a serious warning sign.   
In my case, it was a massive hemorrhagic stroke. A road collapse of epic proportions.   
Why Did This Happen?
That's the question that haunts everyone, right? The "why me?" It's natural, but it's also a rabbit hole. Sometimes, there's a clear cause, sometimes not. Risk factors play a huge role:
•	High blood pressure: This is a big one. It puts extra stress on your blood vessels, making them more likely to burst or clog.
•	Heart disease: Conditions like atrial fibrillation can increase your risk of blood clots.   
•	Smoking: This damages blood vessels and makes them more prone to problems.
•	Diabetes: This can damage blood vessels and increase the risk of clots.
•	Age: The risk of stroke increases as you get older.
•	Family history: If your family has a history of stroke, you might be at higher risk.
But sometimes, even if you do everything "right," it can still happen. That's the cruel randomness of it all.
The Brain Under Siege
A stroke is devastating because brain cells die fast when they're deprived of oxygen. Minutes matter. That's why recognizing the symptoms is crucial.   
FAST: Your Lifeline
Remember this acronym. It can save a life:
•	Face: Ask the person to smile. Does one side droop?   
•	Arms: Ask the person to raise both arms. Does one arm drift down?   
•	Speech: Ask the person to repeat a simple phrase. Is their speech slurred or strange?   
•	Time: If you see any of these signs, call 9-1-1 immediately.   
Time is brain. Every second counts.   
My Brain's Battlefield
In my case, the battleground was the right side of my brain. That meant paralysis on my left side. It's weird how the brain works, right? But that's how it is.   
The stroke also messed with my ability to recognize music and words. They told me I might never be able to tell a hot dog from a hamburger. (Spoiler alert: I can. And I won a hot dog eating contest. So, yeah, miracles happen).

A stroke doesn't just hit the person. It hits the whole family.   
•	Emotions run wild: Shock, disbelief, fear, anxiety, sadness, grief for the person they knew before.   
•	Caregiving becomes a role: Suddenly, spouses, children, they're helping with everything. Feeding, bathing, dressing. It's demanding, physically and emotionally.   
•	Money gets tight: Medical bills, rehab costs, home modifications. If the stroke survivor was the breadwinner, it's even worse.   
•	Roles change: Spouses become caregivers, kids take on more. The person who had the stroke might feel dependent, and that causes tension.   
•	Isolation creeps in: Caregiving takes over, friends drift away. Life revolves around appointments and therapy.   
My stroke didn't just change my life. It changed Lisa's, my parents', everyone's.   
Why Understanding Matters
So, why am I hitting you with all this medical mumbo jumbo? Because knowledge is power, plain and simple.
•	It helps you understand what's happening to your body.   
•	It helps you work with your medical team.   
•	It helps you set realistic goals.   
•	It helps you fight back.
This isn't just about surviving. It's about taking control, as much as you can, in a situation that feels completely out of control. It's about understanding the enemy so you can fight it smarter.
And trust me, you're in for a fight. But you're not in it alone.

Reclaiming Your Legs: A Step-by-Step Approach
(Voice becomes clear and encouraging)
•	Start with the Basics: If you're in a wheelchair, begin with exercises you can do while seated.
o	Heel and toe raises: These simple movements can help activate the muscles in your calves and ankles.
o	Leg lifts: Lifting your legs, one at a time, can help strengthen your hip flexors and quadriceps.
o	Leg circles: Rotating your legs in a circular motion can improve flexibility and range of motion.
•	Gradual Progression: As you gain strength, gradually increase the intensity and duration of your exercises.
o	This might involve increasing the number of repetitions or adding resistance.
•	Weight Bearing: Once you can stand with support, begin weight-bearing exercises.
o	Start by standing for short periods, gradually increasing the time as you get stronger.
•	Walking Practice: Begin with short walks, using a walker or other assistive device for support.
o	Focus on proper form and gait, and gradually increase your distance and speed.
•	Gait Training: Gait training is a specialized form of physical therapy that focuses on improving your walking pattern.
o	This might involve exercises to improve your stride length, balance, and coordination.
•	Assistive Devices: Don't hesitate to use assistive devices like canes, walkers, or AFOs.
o	These tools can provide support and stability, allowing you to move more safely and confidently.
•	Exercises to Enhance Balance and Coordination: Regular physical activities such as walking or stretching can significantly improve mobility and muscle strength.
o	Balance exercises: Exercises like standing on one foot or using a balance board can help improve your stability and coordination.
o	Coordination exercises: Activities that involve hand-eye coordination or fine motor skills can help improve your overall movement control.
o	Regular stretching can enhance flexibility and range of motion. Gentle yoga poses or basic stretches can be beneficial. Remember to breathe deeply and relax into each stretch.
•	Cardiovascular Exercise: Incorporate cardiovascular exercise into your routine, such as using a recumbent bike or elliptical trainer.
o	This can improve your overall fitness and endurance, making it easier to move around.
•	Focus on Form: When performing exercises, focus on proper form and technique.
o	This will help you avoid injuries and maximize the effectiveness of your workouts.
•	Listen to Your Body: Pay attention to your body's signals.
o	Don't push yourself too hard, especially in the early stages of recovery. Rest when you need to, and don't hesitate to take breaks during exercise.
•	Celebrate Progress: Acknowledge and celebrate every improvement, no matter how small.
o	Each step forward is a victory, and it's important to recognize your accomplishments.
